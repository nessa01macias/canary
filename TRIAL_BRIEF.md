## Part 1: What Canary is, in about ninety seconds

Everything you can look up about a place tells you what it is like right now.
Crime rates, prices, walk scores, photos. But nobody moves into the present. Sign
a lease and you are committing to the next three years of that street. Buy, and it
is ten. Lend against it, and it is thirty. The decision is about the future and
every available input describes the past.

Cities publish the raw material for the answer and nobody reads it. Every building
permit, every business that registers or closes, every police report, every noise
complaint, every eviction notice. Millions of rows, updated constantly,
unreadable by a person.

Canary reads them. We turn them into how a neighborhood is actually changing, per
dimension, with a citation on every number, plus a forward layer: what
construction is already approved near a given address, how many homes it adds,
with the permit number attached. We report records that already exist. We never
predict, and we never score a neighborhood as good or bad.

**Where the money is.** People choosing where to live use it free. Revenue comes
from AI products, portals and institutions who need the same intelligence
machine-readable and with guarantees attached.

**How we know the layer does not exist.** We tested the five newest AI models on
136 checkable questions about San Francisco neighborhoods, every answer verified
against the city's own records before any model ran. They scored 25 to 47 percent.
Given one Canary response, 95 to 99. The interesting part is why: these numbers
were never published anywhere, so no model can retrieve or recall them, and no
future model release fixes that. Someone has to compute them first.

That study is a credibility artifact, not a sales lane. We closed the AI-app lane
on 31 July after working out that wrong area answers cost those companies nothing,
so nobody there has a reason to pay. Killing our own favourite lane before
emailing into it for a month is roughly how we like to work.

**What is not proven, which matters more.** The buyer we now think is real is the
regional MLS. The budget line is proven in public, since seven of them have signed
deals with our nearest competitor. Whether they will sign with *us* is completely
untested, and that is the entire point of these three days.

If you want the long version: `CONTEXT.md` is the strategy, `RESEARCH.md` is the
study. Read them in that order, and please come with objections.

## Part 2: What the three days are for

**We are testing one thing about the business** and one thing about us.

About the business: will a regional MLS pay for this. They have the bleed (member
churn after the NAR settlement), they have the budget line (seven public deals
with our nearest competitor), and NAR's own compliance guidance tells their agents
to hand clients exactly what we make. Every part of that is proven except the part
where they say yes to us.

About us: whether the three of us think better together than the two of us do.
That is a real question and it runs in every direction, including yours about us.

**What we are not deciding.** No equity, no titles, no commitment. Three days
tells us whether we want to spend six more weeks together. It does not tell us
enough to build a cap table on, and we would rather not pretend otherwise.

## How we actually work

- **Everything carries a receipt.** A number with no source behind it does not go
  in a document. When we got the Tenderloin crime trend wrong, the underlying
  records showed it, and we published the correction.
- **We write kill criteria before we run the test**, then we do not edit them
  afterwards to make ourselves feel better.
- **A disappointing answer is a result.** If twelve buyer conversations produce
  polite interest and no budget, that is worth more than a good weekend and we
  will say so out loud.
- **We argue directly and it is not personal.** If you think something we said is
  wrong, say it in the room rather than after.
- **Both of us sell and both of us build.** Melany is an engineer who runs GTM.
  Katerina runs product and design and also sells. Please do not assume one of us
  is the technical one, because people do and it gets tedious.

## What actually happens

**The week before:** each of us sends outbound to mid-size regional MLSs that have
not already signed with Local Logic, aiming at executives who answer their own
email. Outbound takes about a week to answer, so if we start on day one we get
replies after everyone has gone home. There is a kit and an email draft in
OUTREACH.md, but bring your own list and write your own version. What you pick and
how you write it is the most useful thing we will see all week.

**Day 1:** read what came back. Take calls. Rewrite the message based on what
actually got replies rather than what we hoped would.

**Day 2:** second wave at a different segment, more calls. By now the same
objection should be showing up twice, which is the useful moment.

**Day 3:** stop selling and decide. Who pays, what did they actually say, what
would make us stop.

**Target:** eight to twelve real conversations across the three of us. Not
friendly coffees. People who could sign.

**The one screen we apply to every conversation:** does this person have budget,
and do they have authority to spend it. Melany's last company died marketing to
people who had the pain and no money, so agreement without a line item gets logged
as a no, not as encouragement.

## What a yes looks like, for us

- You changed our minds about something at least once.
- You brought targets or angles we had not thought of.
- You logged a disappointing answer honestly instead of rounding it up.
- You did the pre-work without being chased.
- You were still useful on day three when everyone was tired.

## What a no looks like, for us

- You waited to be told what to do more than once.
- You turned a soft no into a maybe in the write-up.
- You argued a point and folded the moment we pushed back, or argued it and could
  not let it go once it was decided.
- You did the interesting part and skipped the sending.

## Reasons you might reasonably say no to us

We would rather you knew these now than found them in week five.

- We are pre-revenue and genuinely broke. There is no salary here for a while.
- The core commercial hypothesis is unproven. It is entirely possible these three
  days show us there is no buyer, in which case we will say so.
- We have already built a fair amount, so if this becomes a founding, the split
  will not be three equal ways, and we would rather say that now than negotiate it
  awkwardly later.
- We argue a lot. It is friendly, but it is constant.

## The end of day three

Each of us writes down our answer to one question before anyone says anything out
loud: **do I want to spend the next six weeks with these two.** Then we show each
other at the same time.

We do it that way so nobody anchors on whoever is most enthusiastic, and so a
tiring but good three days does not get talked into being a great one.

Either answer is fine. Agreeing now that either of us can say no at the decision
point, with no explanation owed, is the thing that makes the three days honest.
